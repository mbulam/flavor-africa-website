Adelle Onyango's Legally Clueless is in Forbes

Forbes looks at how she built a business around the show, with radio deals, creator workshops and live shows bringing in what ads alone couldn't.

The Wrap · Local · 25 Nov 2026 · 4 min read

Legally Clueless has made it into Forbes. On Tuesday, 24 November, the magazine published a piece by contributor Losh Moodaley headlined Why The Most Successful Podcasters Don't Rely On Ads Alone, and it uses Adelle Onyango's show as its main example. Its argument is that a podcast can be the heart of a media business without ads paying all the bills, and that she worked this out early.

Adelle shared the news on her own pages. She said she popped the non-alcoholic bubbly for this one, and thanked her team and everyone who has listened, shared a story or come to a live show.

Why ads aren't enough

Podcast advertising is a bigger business than ever. In the US, podcast ad revenue reached about $2.86 billion in 2025, according to the IAB and PwC, nearly double what it was in 2021. But Forbes points out that the money is spread unevenly. More shows are competing for the same ad slots, and the rate advertisers pay for every thousand listens has gone down. The article cites Acast, one of the biggest podcast platforms, which reported that rate falling from $22 in 2021 to $15 in 2025.

For a Kenyan show, the gap is wider still. Forbes cites the 2026 Creative Africa Intelligence Report, which puts podcast ad rates in Kenya at about one-seventh of US levels. Ads alone were never going to carry a business here.

Radio first

Adelle came to podcasting from radio. She hosted the Kiss FM breakfast show until mid-2019, then left to build Legally Clueless, which she had started that March. About a year later, in 2020, Trace FM began airing the show. That made it the first podcast in East Africa to be syndicated on radio, with exclusive episodes going out three times a week, on Mondays, Wednesdays and Fridays.

Forbes reports that the deal paid a monthly retainer plus a share of the ad revenue, which gave the business steady money early on. The show later joined iHeartMedia's Spreaker programme, which carries it to listeners in markets like the US and the UK.

Teaching other creators

The second part of the business is training. Legally Clueless Africa runs digital content workshops built on its own curriculum, and they have trained about 700 people so far. The aim, according to Forbes, is to help African creators get through the slow early months, when listeners drop off and most new shows give up, and to build something that lasts. Guinness has backed one of its projects spotlighting African creatives.

Bringing it into the room

The third part is live events. In 2019, the show toured Kenyan universities to record stories about real campus life. The tours have since gone abroad, with stops in France and Dubai. In September 2026, Legally Clueless took its For Mannerless Women series off the screen for a live gathering in Nairobi, bringing women from the show's community into one room. Forbes notes that she deliberately keeps these rooms small, so women feel safe enough to open up.

The podcast is a tool. It's not the product.

Adelle Onyango, in Forbes

The takeaway

Adelle told Forbes that a lot of the money comes from “the non-sexy, non-creative parts of the business,” and that this is what pays for everything else. She also says there's no single way to do it: each creator can shape a business model that works for them.

Seven years after she started it, the podcast is still at the centre of what Legally Clueless does. The difference is that it now feeds a radio deal, a training curriculum and a live events calendar, and that business is what Forbes chose to write about.
