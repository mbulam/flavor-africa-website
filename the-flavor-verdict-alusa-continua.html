The Verdict

Bien's Alusa Continua: it doesn't all land, and it's worth the noise

Seventeen tracks, a road trip across Kenya and the kind of argument only Bien could start. We've had it on repeat since Friday. Here's where we land.

The Flavor Verdict · 25 Nov 2026 · Album · 7 min read

Bien's second solo album, Alusa Continua, came out on Friday 20 November 2026, and Kenya hasn't stopped arguing about it since. Fans hear proof that Kenyan sounds can travel without being watered down. Critics hear a tour of regions that never settles into a story.

We've had it on repeat for five days. Our call: it doesn't all land, but it's worth the noise. And honestly, only Bien could have started this conversation.

How we got hereA Kenyan record on an Atlanta label

Alusa Continua follows Alusa Why Are You Topless?, Bien's 2023 solo debut. The new title plays on his middle name and the Portuguese rallying cry “a luta continua”, meaning “the struggle continues”. It's a neat fit for a record about resilience and self-belief.

It's also his first album on Quality Control Music, the Atlanta label best known for its rap roster. The deal was announced in October 2026 as a joint venture between Quality Control and Courtesy Of, and it made Bien the label's first African artist. That matters for how people are hearing this album. Many expected a glossy, American-leaning record. They got something else.

Bien made the album by travelling across Kenya. At the album's preview in Nairobi on 7 November, he told guests he had drawn on Luhya isikuti, ohangla, mugithi and Turkana sounds. In a clip shared the same day, he said he met different communities and took in their food, landscapes and culture.

Everything about the country is in the art, in the music.

Bien, on making Alusa Continua

The road to release was long. Lead single All My Enemies Are Suffering came out in 2025, Finale with Tanzania's Alikiba followed, and I'll Be Okay arrived in October 2026 as the first release under the Quality Control deal. The album runs 17 tracks and about 50 minutes.

What it sounds likeHome and away

The short version: Kenya's regional traditions run through a modern pop frame. Apple Music's notes hear benga and Congolese rumba guitar lines next to amapiano log drums and current Afrobeats grooves. Citizen Digital and KBC both describe a mix of Afrobeats, soul, rumba and traditional Kenyan sounds.

What makes it different from his debut is how far he leans into specific communities. Mugithi, ohangla and isikuti aren't background flavour here. They're the reason some of these songs exist. Fans who know vernacular music point to obokano, Kamba-style guitar lines and Kalenjin rhythms.

The features pull the other way, out of Kenya. 2 Chainz brings Atlanta rap, Oxlade brings Nigerian Afrobeats, Joshua Baraka brings Uganda and Alikiba brings Bongo Flava. The album keeps swinging between home and away, and that swing is exactly what people are arguing about.

Track by trackSeventeen songs, one guest list

The tracklist and features below come from the official tracklist. Where a credit or description is on record, we've added it.

  1. Alusa Continua. The title track opens the album.
  2. All My Enemies Are Suffering. The lead single, out in 2025. Apple Music calls it an anthem built on a big choral hook.
  3. Signs with 2 Chainz and Papa Gen Z. The headline crossover: Atlanta rap meets a Kenyan rapper.
  4. Omoka. Les Wanyika share the songwriting credit. Produced by Bien and Remy Baggins, with Benjamin Kabaseke and Ivano Bass on guitars. The song critics have argued about most.
  5. Kaumbete with Coster Ojwang. Apple Music hears sunny guitar riffs over amapiano low end.
  6. Don't Go with Joshua Baraka and The Compozers, the London live band.
  7. Run Like Water.
  8. How Can I Forget with Oxlade and Øntiri. Midtempo Afrobeats.
  9. I'll Be Okay. The October 2026 single, written by Bien, Wuodomollo and Cleopatra Mukula and produced by BGRZ, Remy Baggins and Bien, with Bensoul and Wuodomollo on backing vocals.
  10. XOXO with Ywaya Tajiri. Apple Music calls it a stripped-back acoustic soul ballad.
  11. P.O. Box Kakamega with Bryce One.
  12. Blessed.
  13. Mueni with KayCyy.
  14. Mayolo with Femi One and Papaa Maasai.
  15. Finale with Alikiba. The earlier single, already a fan favourite.
  16. Success with Tulwap Kipsigis and Nono Melodies.
  17. Ah. The closer. Apple Music calls it a plea for pan-African unity.

Read the creditsNames on the page

The most quietly radical thing about Alusa Continua might be its credits page. One widely shared post on release day didn't review a single song. It just asked people to open the credits and give the musicians their flowers.

Omoka is the clearest example. Alongside Bien, the songwriting credits list Les Wanyika, the rumba band behind Sina Makosa. The guitarists who played on it, Benjamin Kabaseke and Ivano Bass, are named too.

Here's why that lands. In May 2022, Les Wanyika said Sauti Sol, Bien's own band, had sampled their songs without permission or royalties. Four years later, the band's name sits in the credits of a Bien song. Whatever happened in between, it's the right ending to that story.

It also matters beyond one song. Kenyan music has fought over uncredited sampling for years, from Les Wanyika to court cases over classic Kikuyu songs. A star album that names its sources this openly sets a bar others will be measured against.

The case forGo global by going home

The strongest argument for this album comes from people who live with vernacular music. One listener who says he listens to a lot of it called Alusa Continua “so good”. He heard obokano, Kamba guitar riffs and Kalenjin tempo working at the core of the songs, not pasted on top. His summary: the best way to go global is to connect with your roots.

That's the bet Bien made. He signed to an Atlanta rap label and, instead of chasing an American sound, went home harder. Capital FM put it well: rather than diluting East African sound for Western algorithms, he exports it straight to the global stage.

Other fans make simpler points that still count. Kenya has a sound, one wrote, and actually many sounds. Another pushed back on the instant takes: a record this long in the making deserves more than a few hours before it gets judged.

And there's the scale of the conversation itself. One listener said they couldn't remember the last time a Kenyan album was discussed this much online. Whatever you think of the music, very few artists can get a whole country arguing about mugithi and ohangla on a Friday.

The case againstA tour, not a story

The critics aren't saying the album is badly made. Their problem is what it adds up to. The most detailed critique argues there's no thread from first song to last. Mugithi gives way to ohangla, then to what that critic calls “Isukha drill”, and nothing connects them as a story. The result, in that critic's words, sounds “like a DJ set”.

The sharper charge is about respect. Some listeners hear regional sounds used for colour rather than lived in, a sampler of Kenya for people who've never been. One critic called it tokenistic. Another scored it 4 out of 10 and said parts of it could offend the communities it borrows from.

Omoka has taken the heaviest hits. Critics who love mugithi hear its take on the genre as a caricature, and one called it “meme music”. Even a fan who loves the song wished it had featured an actual mugithi artist.

There's also a producer argument. Some listeners wanted a Kenyan producer leading the culture-heavy songs. Others replied that few local producers could have pulled off what was done here. Wuodomollo was named as one Kenyan who could have done it, and he's already on the album as a writer and backing vocalist on I'll Be Okay.

The bigger questionLocal sound, global stage

Under all of this sits a question one listener asked out loud: where should an artist draw the line between a local sound and an international one? Another asked what “international sound” even means. Is it singing in English over R&B and Afrobeats?

Alusa Continua answers that in a way some people didn't expect. Bien signed to Quality Control and made his most Kenyan record yet. That frustrated listeners who wanted an American-leaning album. It also frustrated listeners who think he only went home for the visuals.

We think both camps are partly right, and that's why the album matters. The most useful critique isn't “don't touch our sounds”. It's the fan who loved Omoka and still wanted a mugithi artist on it. If you borrow a community's sound, give its people a seat at the table. The credits show Bien already understands that. The next album should go further.

Our verdictPlay it loud

Alusa Continua doesn't hang together the way a great album should. Seventeen tracks and this many guests pull it in a lot of directions, and the critics are right that it rarely tells one story from start to finish.

But it's the biggest swing a Kenyan artist has taken in a while. It puts mugithi, ohangla and isikuti on a Quality Control release. It names its sources in the credits. And it has the whole country listening closely to its own sounds and arguing about them.

It doesn't all land. It still matters. Play it loud, then pick a side.
